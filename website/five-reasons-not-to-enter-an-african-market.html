Five reasons not to enter an African market

By Tonderai Tarusenga · 3 November 2026

Most entry plans are written to show why the answer is yes. I start by asking what would make it no. These are the five things I check first. If any of them is true, I stop there.

1. You're creating demand, not meeting it

Educating a market is slow and expensive. If your case rests on "people will want this once they see it", you are paying to build the category for everyone else. Look for markets where people already pay, or already do the job badly, and where switching costs are low.

This is why a greenfield market is overrated. No incumbents often means no proven demand.

2. A funded incumbent owns the market, and you have no structural moat

Being faster, better or cheaper is not a moat. A well-funded incumbent with network effects will match you on features and outspend you on price. Unless you have something that can't be copied in 6 to 12 months, such as distribution, supply lock-in, a cost advantage or a regulatory edge, you are betting on capital you don't have.

3. The rules are unclear

In most markets, unclear regulation means "not allowed yet", not "allowed". Regulation sets who can operate, how you can pay people and what you can charge. Find out before you build, not after your first notice.

4. Year-one revenue is under US$100,000

Not the total market. The realistic revenue you can win in the first year given competition and complexity. If that number can't support a small team, the market is too small for now. This is why the city matters: Johannesburg is not Kimberley.

5. You haven't talked to real customers

Friends and surveys don't count. The test is people who would actually transact. Run the service manually if you have to: be the supply and the demand yourself for a few weeks and see whether the flow works.

What to do instead

If you hit one of these, you don't necessarily drop the idea. You change the plan: a smaller first city, a partner who already has the supply, or a different model. The point is to find out before the budget is spent.
